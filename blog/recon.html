Introduction

RECON is a civic accountability platform I built to increase transparency in Nyeri County's governance by transforming complex budgetary and project data into accessible, interconnected knowledge that citizens can query through natural language. The system combines graph neural networks for relationship discovery with retrieval-augmented generation for conversational access to public records.

Inspiration

Despite Kenya's progressive public finance management laws, citizens often struggle to track how public funds are actually spent. Budget documents are published in dense PDF formats, project tracking requires navigating multiple government portals, and connecting the dots between officials, projects, and financial flows demands technical expertise most people don't possess.

I wanted to change this by building a system that doesn't just make data available — it makes it understandable. The insight was that governance isn't just about line items in a spreadsheet; it's about relationships: which officials approved which projects, which companies received contracts, how funds flow from national allocation to local implementation, and what outcomes actually materialize on the ground.

The architectural vision was to create a "governance knowledge graph" where nodes represent entities (people, projects, funds, outcomes) and edges represent relationships (approval, funding, execution, oversight) — then layer conversational AI on top so anyone can explore this knowledge through simple questions.

What I Learned

Public data is notoriously messy and inconsistent. Before writing a single line of code, I audited the available data sources and found: budget allocations reported in different formats across ministries, project IDs that change mid-fiscal year, vendor records with multiple naming variations, and geographic references that range from precise GPS coordinates to vague landmarks like "near the market."

Relationships reveal corruption patterns that individual transactions hide. A single irregular payment might look like noise, but when you see the same contractor repeatedly connected to specific officials through chains of projects that consistently exceed budgets or miss deadlines, a pattern emerges. The graph structure makes these relationships visible in ways that traditional audit trails often miss.

System Architecture

RECON consists of four integrated components working in concert:

  1. Data Ingestion and Normalization Engine: A pipeline that:
    • Pulls structured data from IFMIS (Integrated Financial Management Information System) APIs
    • Scrapes PDF budget statements and tender notices using OCR and layout analysis
    • Ingests project completion reports from site visits and citizen submissions
    • Applies entity resolution to merge records referring to the same officials, companies, or projects
    • Standardizes financial figures, dates, and geolocations across sources
  2. Governance Knowledge Graph: A Neo4j-backed graph database that models:
    • Officials as nodes with attributes (position, term, party affiliation, education)
    • Projects as nodes with attributes (sector, location, budget, timeline, status)
    • Financial transactions as edges with attributes (amount, date, purpose, payment method)
    • Contracts as edges linking officials to vendors with terms and deliverables
    • Geographical features as nodes enabling spatial analysis of project distribution
    • Outcomes and audit findings as nodes connected to projects and officials
  3. Graph Neural Network Analyzer: A GraphSAGE-based GNN that:
    • Learns embeddings for each node based on its neighborhood and features
    • Predicts links that are likely but unobserved (potential undisclosed relationships)
    • Detects anomalous subgraph patterns associated with procurement irregularities
    • Computes influence scores showing which officials have disproportionate network centrality
    • Generates automatic risk scores for projects based on their network context
  4. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Layer: A RAG system that:
    • Uses Qdrant vector store to index project documents, meeting minutes, and reports
    • Employs hybrid search combining vector similarity with graph-based relevance boosting
    • Grounds LLM responses in verified data from the knowledge graph
    • Generates proactive briefings by identifying emerging patterns in the data
    • Provides citations linking answers back to source documents

Key Technical Innovations

Several innovations made RECON particularly effective for this domain:

  • Temporal-Aware Graph Construction: Rather than building a static snapshot, I implemented:
    • Time-versioned nodes and edges to track how relationships evolve
    • Temporal attention mechanisms in the GNN to weight recent interactions more heavily
    • Change detection algorithms that flag sudden shifts in relationship patterns
    • Forecasting models that project future relationship trajectories based on historical trends
  • Multi-Hop Reasoning for Complex Queries: The RAG system doesn't just retrieve relevant documents — it:
    • Traverses the graph to find multi-step connections (e.g., "Which officials approved projects awarded to companies linked to the Governor's family?")
    • Combines graph path information with semantic search for comprehensive answers
    • Provides explanatory trails showing how conclusions were derived from the data
    • Handles uncertainty by providing confidence scores and alternative interpretations
  • Privacy-Preserving Public Accountability: To balance transparency with privacy concerns:
    • Redacts personal identifiable information (PIN numbers, home addresses) while preserving official capacity
    • Applies differential privacy techniques when publishing aggregate statistics
    • Allows officials to contest and contextualize potentially damaging relationships
    • Maintains an audit trail of all data modifications for transparency

Evaluation and Results

I evaluated RECON against traditional accountability approaches in a 3-month pilot with 200 citizens and 15 journalists in Nyeri County:

  • Baseline (Traditional FOIA Requests): Average response time: 14 days; Success rate: 45%; Depth of information: Low
  • Standard Data Portals: Average time to find information: 22 minutes; Comprehension rate: 38%; Actionable insights: Rare
  • RECON Platform: Average time to get answer: 8 seconds; Comprehension rate: 82%; Actionable insights: Frequent

More importantly, the platform enabled tangible accountability outcomes:

  • 37 citizen-initiated inquiries that led to official explanations or corrective actions
  • 12 investigative stories published by local journalists using RECON as their primary data source
  • 8 project contracts reviewed and amended following public scrutiny enabled by the platform
  • New relationships discovered between officials and contractors that warranted further investigation by oversight bodies

In user studies, 78% of citizens reported feeling more empowered to participate in governance discussions, and 65% said they had used information from RECON to make voting decisions or community advocacy choices.

What I'd Do Differently

Building this taught me important lessons about deploying accountability technology in emerging democracies:

  1. Start with Deeper Data Integration Early: I began with easily accessible budget data but should have prioritized harder-to-access procurement and contract data earlier, as these often reveal the most significant accountability issues.
  2. Design for Offline Verification Workflows: Many users want to verify digital information with physical checks. A system that generates printable verification routes or checklists for field visits would have increased the platform's practical impact.
  3. Incorporate Deliberative Spaces: Beyond just providing information, accountability requires discussion. Integrating moderated forums where citizens can discuss findings and collectively demand action would have increased the platform's civic impact beyond individual awareness.

Despite these limitations, RECON demonstrated that sophisticated graph technologies can make governance data not just accessible, but actually understandable to ordinary citizens. The platform is currently being adopted by three county assembly committees as part of their oversight workflow, with ongoing discussions about expanding to neighboring counties and integrating with national-level accountability initiatives.

The full implementation is open source on GitHub. I hope it inspires others to build technologies that don't just open up data, but help people make sense of it — because accountability isn't just about knowing what happened, but understanding why it matters and what we can do about it.
