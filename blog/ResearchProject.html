When I began my final year research project at Dedan Kimathi University of Technology, I wanted to tackle a problem that blended traditional civil engineering with emerging computational methods. Hydrogen generation in reinforced concrete structures—a slow-burning safety hazard where corrosive environments trigger chemical reactions that produce flammable gas—felt like the perfect intersection. It’s not just theoretically interesting; in coastal regions of Kenya, chloride-induced corrosion poses real risks to infrastructure longevity and public safety.

Why This Problem?

Most existing studies on hydrogen generation in concrete focus on either the chemistry (theoretical reaction rates) or extreme lab conditions (accelerated corrosion tests). Few bridge the gap to real-world structural behavior where variables like water-content ratio (W/C) and concrete cover thickness interact in complex ways. I saw an opportunity to use machine learning not as a black-box predictor, but as a tool to uncover non-linear relationships in sparse, noisy experimental data.

Experimental Setup

I prepared 48 concrete specimens with varying W/C ratios (0.4, 0.5, 0.6) and cover depths (15mm, 20mm, 25mm, 30mm), exposed them to simulated marine environments (3.5% NaCl solution), and measured hydrogen evolution weekly over 180 days. The dataset was small but rich—each specimen became a time-series of electrochemical potential, corrosion rate, and hydrogen flux measurements.

From Physics-Informed Features to ML Insights

Instead of feeding raw sensor data into a neural net, I engineered features based on corrosion science:

  • Chloride penetration depth modeled via Fick’s second law
  • Hydrogen generation rate tied to iron corrosion kinetics (Faraday’s laws)
  • Interaction terms between W/C ratio and cover depth (since denser concrete slows chloride ingress)
This reduced the dimensionality and gave the model physics-informed priors. A gradient-boosted regressor (XGBoost) outperformed pure neural networks here—it handled the small dataset well and provided feature importance scores that aligned with domain knowledge.

What the Model Revealed

The most surprising insight? Concrete cover depth had a non-linear protective effect. Beyond 25mm, diminishing returns kicked in sharply—each additional millimeter of cover reduced hydrogen generation by less than 5%, whereas between 15-25mm, the same increment yielded 15-20% reduction. This suggests that for coastal structures in Kenya, specifying cover depths beyond 25mm may not be cost-effective; resources might be better spent on improving concrete density (lower W/C ratio) or using corrosion inhibitors.

Challenges and Limitations

Working with real concrete data is messy. Specimen variability (aggregate distribution, micro-cracks) introduced noise that no amount of preprocessing could fully eliminate. I addressed this by:

  • Using ensemble methods to reduce variance
  • Reporting prediction intervals alongside point estimates
  • Acknowledging that the model captures trends, not exact physical laws
    • Also, the 180-day test period—while standard for accelerated corrosion—may not capture long-term passivation effects. Future work should extend monitoring to 1+ year.

    Lessons Learned

    This project taught me that ML in civil engineering isn’t about replacing domain expertise—it’s about augmenting it. The best models emerged when I constrained the hypothesis space with physical laws, letting the algorithm fine-tune parameters within scientifically plausible bounds. I’d do two things differently next time:

    1. Start with a digital twin approach: calibrate a physics-based model first, then use ML to correct systematic errors
    2. Partner with a materials lab earlier to access more sophisticated characterization tools (like SEM imaging of corrosion products)

    The code and dataset are available on GitHub. While this was an academic project, I hope the methodology—blending mechanistic modeling with targeted ML—can inspire others working on infrastructure durability challenges in resource-constrained settings.
